## Part 1: findings

### 1. Rhythm carries "how far off" better than pitch
- **Pulse rate is a robust urgency cue.** Gonzalez, Lewis & Baldwin (2012) found pulse rate "may be a more reliable and robust parameter for conveying levels of urgency than frequency" ([Georgia Tech repository](https://repository.gatech.edu/entities/publication/441d087b-8b3e-4572-b046-07096dcc1b60)). In Hellier & Edworthy's work, speed and repetition both raised perceived urgency ([Haas & Edworthy record](https://researchportal.plymouth.ac.uk/en/publications/designing-urgency-into-auditory-warnings-using-pitch-speed-and-lo/)).
- **Parking sensors** split distance into bands: slow, medium and fast intermittent beeps, then a continuous tone at about 35 cm. The beep rate also rises within each band ([Mazda MX-5 manual](https://owners-manual.mazda.com/gen/en/mx-5/mx-5_8ja8ee19k/contents/05200104.html)). Mazda also mutes a beep that has stayed in one zone for 6 s, which is a deliberate anti-nuisance rule. We should **not** copy the continuous final stage, because a continuous tone is what was rejected.
- **Variometers** use a pulsed tone whose repetition rate shows climb rate, and its pitch can show the value too ([EP0024904A2](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP0024904A2/en)). Many have a silent dead band around zero, with a separate low or stuttering tone for sink ([DIY vario write-up](https://jpralves.net/post/2020/04/09/acoustic-variometer-ultra-sensible-barometer.html), [Wings & Wheels vario 101](https://wingsandwheels.com/blog/post/variometer-101)). Pilots fly for hours with these, and that dead band is our "in band = silent".
- **Geiger counters:** the click rate tracks intensity directly, so you can monitor it in the background while your attention is elsewhere ([CU Boulder demo](https://physicslabs.colorado.edu/demos/modern-physics/nuclear-physics/particle-detectors/geiger-counter/)).

### 2. Short, decaying pulses are easier on the ear and on the walls
- **Temporal integration:** loudness holds roughly constant down to about 200 ms and then drops fast. In Munson's data a 60-phon tone shortened to 10 ms falls to about 35 phons ([SFU handbook, Loudness](https://www.sfu.ca/sonic-studio-webdav/cmns/Handbook5/handbook/Loudness.html)). The level needed to make a 5 ms tone as loud as a 200 ms one is about 10–17 dB higher, depending on level ([ASA 1993 abstract](https://auditory.org/asamtgs/asa93ott/4aPP/4aPP8.html)). A low-duty pulse train therefore radiates far less energy than a continuous tone of similar salience. Average power relative to continuous is 10·log10(duty): 20 % duty gives −7 dB and 10 % gives −10 dB.
- **Envelope:** Edworthy's 2025 review says auditory icons, which are sounds like everyday ones with dense and complex spectra, are easier to learn and localise and are "less tiring and irritating" than tonal alarms ([Forum Acusticum 2025 PDF](https://dael.euracoustics.org/confs/fa2025/data/articles/000375.pdf), [Edworthy, Talbot & Martin 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12106860/)). IEC 60601-1-8 Amendment 2 (2020) moved from note melodies to auditory icons ([PUI Audio summary](https://puiaudio.com/news/key-updates-to-iec-60601-1-8-what-engineers-need-to-know)). A McMaster thesis links the flat envelopes of the IEC melodies to annoyance and tests percussive ones instead ([thesis PDF](https://macsphere.mcmaster.ca/bitstream/11375/24878/2/Sharmila%20MSc%20Thesis%20FINAL.pdf)). The design takeaway is a fast attack, an exponential decay and no flat sustain where possible.
- **Onset:** steep, loud onsets cause startle. A secondary source cites Sorkin (1987): keep onsets below 10 dB/ms, and use about 1 dB/ms for sounds meant to alert without startling ([Taylor & Francis topic page](https://taylorandfrancis.com/knowledge/Engineering_and_technology/Biomedical_engineering/Startle_response)). Our pulses are quiet, at roughly 40–50 dB SPL at the ear, so 1–6 ms attacks are a reasonable trade between crispness and onset rate. The ticks (A and C) are the steepest. If they feel "spitty", lengthen the attack to 3 ms.
- **IEC 60601-1-8 pulse rules** are for medical alarms, not feedback, but they're a useful ceiling. Each pulse has a fundamental of 150–1000 Hz and at least four harmonics in 300–4000 Hz, and the standard defines pulse counts per priority and limits on level differences ([MDDI](https://www.mddionline.com/components/audible-alarms-in-medical-equipment), [Mallory/MSP](https://mspindy.com/technical-articles/using-audible-alarms-in-medical-equipment-iec-60601-1-8/)). Our error sound should sit *below* "medium priority" in urgency, which means slower pulses and fewer harmonics.

### 3. Modulation rate decides the character: rhythm, purr or rasp
- Fluctuation strength, the slow swelling and siren-like quality, peaks near **4 Hz**. Roughness, a harsh rasp, peaks near **70 Hz**, roughly in the 15/20–300 Hz range ([Siemens: fluctuation strength and roughness](https://community.sw.siemens.com/articles/en_US/Knowledge/sound-modulation-metrics-fluctuation-strength-and-roughness)). In the Zwicker & Fastl annoyance model, roughness and fluctuation add to loudness-based annoyance. Their weight is relatively larger for quiet sounds ([MetricGate summary](https://metricgate.com/docs/psychoacoustic-annoyance/); third-party, check against Zwicker & Fastl before quoting).
- Inference (not a published finding): foot corrections happen at about 1–4 Hz, so the old pitch-follows-error tone wobbled in the 4 Hz fluctuation zone. That made it sound like a siren that never stops.
- Practical bands: **3–12 pulses/s** reads as rhythm, which is the "tr-tr-tr" the user asked for. **10–25 Hz** reads as a purr or roll. Avoid **30–150 Hz** AM, which is rough and buzzy.
- **Sharpness** weights energy above 3 kHz by up to 4× (same MetricGate summary), so keep pulse energy below about 2.5 kHz. Real parking-sensor piezos sit at 2–4 kHz, which is why they're shrill. Our "parking sensor" uses 784 Hz.

### 4. What travels through the house
- The **mass law** says wall transmission loss rises about 6 dB per doubling of frequency, and real partitions manage about 4–5 dB. At the lowest frequencies stiffness and resonances govern instead, which is why bass passes through walls ([Engineers Universe simulator](https://engineersuniverse.com/labs/engineering/acoustical-engineering/noise-transmission-through-walls), [Science Insights: transmission loss](https://scienceinsights.org/what-is-transmission-loss-and-how-is-it-measured/)). Going from 220 Hz to 880 Hz, two octaves, buys about 8–12 dB of extra wall loss.
- The ear is less sensitive at low frequencies when the level is low ([equal-loudness contours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal-loudness_contour)). A low hum therefore has to be played *louder* to be heard at the desk, and that extra energy is exactly what leaks out. Small desktop and laptop speakers also roll off below about 150 Hz.
- Sweet spot: fundamentals of **400–1200 Hz**, energy rolled off above about 2.5 kHz, low duty cycle and a low level. Headphones remove the problem altogether. The app should give the error cue its **own volume**, separate from the chime.

### 5. Motor-learning angle: silence in band is right, and feedback should fade
- **Bandwidth feedback** gives feedback only when the error is outside a tolerance window. It improves consistency, and some retention benefits lasted a week in later studies ([Sherwood 1988](https://vivo.colorado.edu/display/pubid_105370); [bandwidth KR persists](https://repositorio.ufmg.br/items/d45b1748-dd9c-4004-b123-6cfe07cd166a/full)). This is the same design as "silent in band".
- **Concurrent feedback** helps acquisition but can create dependence (the guidance hypothesis). Auditory concurrent feedback has sometimes held up better in no-feedback retention than visual feedback ([Sigrist et al. 2013 review](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-012-0333-8); [Dyer et al. 2017](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5486555/)). A rowing-type *error* sonification study found no learning gain over terminal feedback ([BIO Web of Conferences 2011](https://www.bio-conferences.org/articles/bioconf/abs/2011/01/bioconf_skills_00098/bioconf_skills_00098.html)). Plan an option to fade or thin the error cue later, which fits the planned "blind" mode SCT-072.
- In sonification practice pitch is by far the most-used mapping, and proper evaluation of mappings is rare ([Dubus & Bresin 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3866150/)). Being the most common mapping doesn't make it the right one here.

### 6. Should the pulse encode magnitude and/or direction?
- **Magnitude: yes, through rate or texture, not pitch.** It follows the parking-sensor and Geiger logic and the urgency evidence above. Map *distance beyond the band edge* `d = |err| − tol`, not the raw error, so the cue starts gently at the edge. A saturating curve gives resolution near the band: `k = min(d/30, 1)^0.6`. Update the rate per pulse (sample and hold), so the rhythm never glides.
- **Direction: optional.** The screen already shows over and under. Absolute pitch judgement of single tones is poor, at about 5–6 categories even for trained listeners (Pollack 1952, summarised in [Miller 1956](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magical_Number_Seven,_Plus_or_Minus_Two)). A *continuous* pitch map is therefore hard to read, while **two fixed pitches** (F) or a **contour** (G: falling pair = ease off, rising pair = push) can be learned. Recommendation: ship size-by-rate as the default, and offer direction as a setting for Trace drills and blind mode.
- **Anti-chatter:** use about 0.4 %-pt hysteresis on *re-entry* only. Leaving the band triggers instantly, so there's no added latency, and edge jitter doesn't stutter.

## Part 2: candidates (parameters as implemented on the page)

Levels use the same units as the chime (chime peak = 0.30 on a sine). Noise is white noise uniform in ±1 *before* filtering, and its "level" multiplies that. Levels were roughly **energy-matched** to about 0.022 RMS over 200 ms at a medium error (chime ≈ 0.036). They were matched by calculation, not by ear, so trim after listening. `k = min(d/30,1)^0.6` with `d = |err| − 5`. Envelopes use a linear attack, then a one-pole exponential decay with time constant τ (−60 dB at about 6.9 τ). In Rust that's `env *= exp(-1/(τ·fs))` per sample.

One-line rationale, pros and cons:
- **A Rolled "tr"**: the user's "tr-tr-tr" taken literally. Noise has no pitch, so it can't sound like a beeper. The band-limited 250–1000 Hz band gives low sharpness and leaks less than bass. *Con:* noise can read as "static" on poor speakers.
- **B Wood tok**: an auditory-icon-like knock with a percussive envelope and about 10 % duty, the most "physical" of the set. *Con:* without rate scaling it carries no magnitude, and a steady 5/s can sound like a metronome.
- **C Geiger tick**: the most natural density cue, and its random timing never sounds mechanical. *Con:* the random gaps can make you briefly unsure whether you're still out of band, and at high density it crackles.
- **D Soft blip**: the plainest option, a sibling of the chime. *Con:* tonal beeps are the "beepy" family the user is moving away from.
- **E Parking sensor**: understood instantly, with rate showing size. *Con:* the car association, flat-top beeps carry more energy, and it gets insistent at 11/s.
- **F Variometer**: the most information (size and direction), proven over long glider and paraglider flights. *Con:* it's tonal, and the over/under pitches take a few minutes to learn.
- **G Tick-tock pair**: direction by contour, which is easier than absolute pitch, and a musical feel. *Con:* no magnitude, it's a slow update (2.5 pairs/s), and it's the most "musical", which can wear.
- **H Low purr**: engine-like and on theme for racing, a smooth flutter. *Con:* the lowest fundamental is the worst for walls and laptop speakers, and it's the closest to the rejected continuous tone.

## Implementation notes for the Rust synth
- Use a per-voice countdown `samples_to_next`. On band **exit**, set it to 0 so the first pulse lands in the current buffer, which meets the ≤20 ms requirement. On **re-entry**, stop triggering and multiply the output by a gate that falls with τ = 6 ms (click-free even mid-pulse). The ticks would decay on their own anyway. Flat-top voices (E, F, H) need the gate.
- At each trigger, read the latest error and compute the next interval and the pulse parameters from it (sample and hold per pulse). No allocation is needed: use fixed voice pools (≤4 overlapping taps or modes).
- **Noise:** xorshift32 → f32 in ±1. **Filters:** RBJ cookbook biquads, with the BPF in the "constant 0 dB peak gain" form. Web Audio's `lowpass` Q is in dB, so the page passes −3.01 dB to get Butterworth 0.707. **Saw:** sum 5 sines (no aliasing). **Hann grain:** `0.5 − 0.5·cos(2π·x)` over the on-portion.
- **Geiger:** `interval = −ln(1−U)/rate`, clamped. **Variometer and tick-tock direction:** `sign(pedal − target)`. Positive means too much pedal.
- **Hysteresis:** leave when `|err| > tol`, re-enter when `|err| < tol − 0.4`.
- The page's master volume is `2·v²` (default v = 50 % → 0.5). The Rust app should expose a separate error-cue volume.
